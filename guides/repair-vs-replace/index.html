Guide

Repair or replace that appliance?

Get a diagnosis first, then compare the quoted repair with the cost and remaining life of a new machine. This page does not invent average Phoenix repair prices.

Start with a diagnosis, not a guess

A “click” from a dryer or a warm refrigerator can have more than one cause. An independent technician can often tell you whether the job is a modest part and labor bill or a major sealed-system or control-board repair. Several shops in our Phoenix directory publish a service-call fee that is waived or applied if you approve the work — ask about the current policy when you book.

Questions worth asking

  1. What failed, and is the part still available?
  2. What will the repair cost, including labor, and what is warranted after the visit?
  3. How old is the appliance, and has it needed other repairs recently?
  4. If you replaced it, what would a comparable new unit cost, installed?
  5. Would a newer model use meaningfully less energy in your home?

A common rule of thumb — and its limits

Consumer writers often suggest repairing when the quote is well under half the price of a comparable new appliance, and replacing when the machine is near the end of a typical life and the repair is a large share of that replacement cost. Treat this as a starting point, not a law. A $400 repair on a well-maintained refrigerator can be sensible; the same bill on a washer that has already failed twice this year may not be.

We are not publishing made-up “average repair costs in Phoenix.” Those figures vary by brand, part, and travel, and they go stale. Use the quote in your hand.

When replacement is often the better path

  • The technician cannot get a part, or the appliance uses a refrigerant or control system that is no longer practical to service.
  • The box, tub, or cabinet itself is rusted or damaged.
  • You already have a written diagnosis, and stacking another major repair on a short remaining life does not make sense to you.

If the refrigerator already failed

Keep the doors closed. Food-safety time limits depend on the appliance, how full it is, and the room temperature — which in a Phoenix summer can be unforgiving. Use a thermometer if you have one, and follow current food-safety guidance from a public-health source such as FoodSafety.gov or USDA, not a memory of a rule of thumb.

Talk to a local tech

If you are in the Valley, browse the Phoenix directory and call a shop that lists your appliance. Ask for a diagnosis and a written or on-site price before you decide.
